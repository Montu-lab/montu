This is the right instinct, and it's the thing that converts your "model-agnostic" criterion from a slogan into architecture. Let me make it concrete, because "recursive AI" can stay a buzzword if we're not careful.

The recursion is actually three nested loops, each feeding the next.

**Loop one: the system learns the crew.** Every session refines a living model of that specific group: who instigates, who flakes, which games produce the legendary nights versus the forgettable ones, what time windows actually work, which moments the crew retells and which they ignore, the narrative roles that emerged (the betrayer, the clutch, the peacemaker). Then it observes what happened and updates. The ritual gets deeper, which generates richer behavior, which deepens the model. That's the recursion at the crew level.

**Loop two: the system learns what makes each person feel irreplaceable, individually.** This is the "specifically and directly" part, and it's not feed-style personalization. It's personalization of *mattering*. It learns that you light up when your strategic call wins the night, while your friend lights up when his joke becomes lore, while another needs to be the organizer to feel load-bearing. Then it quietly engineers more of each person's version: surfacing "your call won this" in the recap, referencing the flake in the lore so missing a night costs him the story instead of a guilt trip. Over months it accumulates a model of your friendships that rivals your own understanding, and spends it entirely on deepening the ritual. Nobody is building personal superintelligence for friendship. Everyone's building it for productivity.

**Loop three: the system learns about itself, across crews.** This is the meta-loop and the real moat. It runs evals on its own interventions: did this recap get shared or ignored? Did this nudge bring the flake back or annoy him? Did the ritual survive the month? The outcomes refine a cross-crew playbook of ritual mechanics, so a brand-new crew on day one benefits from everything learned from ten thousand dead and living rituals. A competitor can copy the features. They can't copy the playbook, because the playbook only exists inside the loop.

**Then the model-swap harness, which is your "constantly mod" point.** The product is built as a harness around models, not on top of one. Lore-keeping, recap generation, nudge writing, moment detection: each is a module with a clean interface. When a better model drops, you swap it in and everything gets better overnight, because the crew models and the lore persist while the intelligence running on them upgrades. Today an LLM writes the recap from session notes. Tomorrow a video model cuts the trailer. Next, real-time multimodal catches the moments automatically from the stream. Every lab announcement is a free upgrade instead of an extinction event. The value was never in any single capability. It's in the accumulated relationship state, and labs can't download your crew's inside jokes.

Three honest caveats, because this is where it usually dies:

First, your cold-start question stands. Recursion needs data and day-one crews have no lore. The answer is humility sequencing: at first the product just keeps the record faithfully and observes. It earns the right to intervene. The cross-crew playbook seeds the defaults ("crews like yours usually..."), but the system shouldn't act clever until it has something real to be clever about.

Second, creepiness. A system that models your friendships this deeply can feel surveillant. The design constraint: everything it learns must be legible and in service of the crew. The "previously on" is the model's visible surface: the crew sees what the system noticed, and it feels like being known, not watched. The moment it does something the crew wouldn't endorse on reflection, it's over the line and probably dead.

Third, the "mod" discipline is engineering, not magic. It means modular architecture plus eval harnesses: does the new model actually write better recaps? Prove it before swapping. And the willingness to throw away your own clever prompting when the base model gets better. Most teams fail here because they hard-code around one model's quirks and then can't move.

One line for the whole thing: the product is a **ritual intelligence** that compounds. The models are rented. The relationships are owned. And the system gets better at getting better, which is the only lead that widens instead of decaying.
